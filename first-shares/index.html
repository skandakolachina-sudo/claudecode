Lesson 1 of 14 · 8 min

What a stock actually is

By the end, you can explain what you own when you buy a share and the two ways it can make you money.

A stock is a piece of ownership in a company. Each piece is called a share. If a company is split into 1,000,000 shares and you own 10, you own 0.001% of it. That's tiny, but it's real: you're a part-owner, called a shareholder.

Big companies do the same thing on a huge scale. The first time a company sells shares to the public is called an IPO (initial public offering). After that, its shares trade between investors on a stock exchange, like the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) or the Nasdaq.

Good to know

When you buy a share in an app, you are almost always buying it from another investor, not from the company. The company got its money at the IPO. Your purchase just changes who owns that share.

Every public company has a ticker symbol, a short code for its stock. AAPL is Apple, KO is Coca-Cola, and NKE is Nike.

Two ways a stock can make you money

  1. The price goes up. Buy a share at $40, sell it at $50, and you made $10. That profit is a capital gain. Until you sell, it's an unrealized gain. It only exists on paper and can disappear.
  2. Dividends. Some companies pay part of their profits to shareholders in cash, usually every three months. That payment is a dividend. Other companies keep all their profits to grow faster, so they pay none.

How you can lose money

If the price falls below what you paid and you sell, that's a capital loss. If a company goes bankrupt, its stock can drop to $0. When you simply buy shares with your own money, the most you can lose is what you put in. Lesson 9 covers the ways people lose more than that, so you can avoid them.

Lesson 2 of 14 · 9 min

How the market moves

By the end, you can explain why prices change, what market cap tells you, and what people mean by "the market."

A stock's price is the last price a buyer and a seller agreed on. If more people want to buy than sell at the current price, buyers have to offer more, and the price rises. If more people want to sell, sellers have to accept less, and it falls.

Bid, ask and spread

At any moment, the bid is the highest price a buyer is offering, and the ask is the lowest price a seller will accept. The gap between them is the spread. Popular stocks have spreads of a penny or two. Small, rarely traded stocks can have wide spreads, which quietly cost you money every time you buy or sell.

What actually moves prices

In the long run, a stock follows the company's profits. In the short run, it follows expectations.

  • Earnings reports. Every three months, public companies report their sales and profits in an earnings report. The price reacts to how the results compare with what investors expected.
  • News. New products, lawsuits, a new CEO, a scandal.
  • The economy and interest rates. When interest rates rise, safe savings and bonds pay more, so stocks look less attractive by comparison. Prices often dip.
  • Mood. Fear and excitement can push prices far above or below what a business is worth, for a while.

Market cap: how big is the company?

The share price alone doesn't tell you how big or valuable a company is. For that, use market capitalization, usually called market cap.

FormulaMarket cap = share price × shares outstanding
CompanyShare priceSharesMarket cap
Company A$50010 million$5 billion
Company B$501 billion$50 billion

B's shares are cheaper, but B is worth ten times more. A $500 stock isn't "more expensive" than a $50 stock in any way that matters, and with fractional shares (Lesson 3) you can buy $5 of either. Investors often group companies by size: large-cap (over about $10 billion), mid-cap (about $2 to $10 billion) and small-cap (under about $2 billion). The cutoffs vary a little by source. Huge, well-established companies are often called blue-chip stocks.

"The market" usually means an index

An index tracks a group of stocks to show how part of the market is doing.

  • S&P 500: about 500 large US companies, weighted by size. This is what most people mean by "the market."
  • Dow Jones Industrial Average: 30 large companies. Old and famous, but small.
  • Nasdaq Composite: thousands of stocks on the Nasdaq exchange, heavy in technology.

You can't buy an index directly, but you can buy a fund that copies one. That's Lesson 5.

US stock exchanges are open 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time, Monday to Friday, except market holidays. Some brokers allow trading outside those hours, but there are fewer buyers and sellers then, and spreads are wider.

Stock splits

In a stock split, a company turns each share into several. In a 2-for-1 split, your 1 share at $100 becomes 2 shares at $50. You own exactly the same value. Think of cutting a pizza into more slices: there's no extra pizza.

Your broker

To buy stock, you need a brokerage account: an account at a company that places trades for you. Most large US brokers now charge $0 commission to trade US stocks and ETFs online. Under 18? Lesson 8 covers the accounts you can use.

Order types

OrderWhat it tells the brokerUse it when
Market order"Buy (or sell) right now at the best price available."Getting the trade done now matters most, and the stock trades a lot.
Limit order"Buy only at $X or lower," or "Sell only at $X or higher."The price matters most. It might never fill.
Stop-loss order"If the price falls to $X, sell at market."You want an automatic exit. In a fast drop, it can sell well below $X.
Stop-limit order"If the price hits $X, place a limit order at $Y."You want a trigger and a price floor, and accept it might not sell.

A day order expires when the market closes. A good-'til-canceled (GTC) order stays open until it fills or you cancel it. Most brokers cancel GTC orders automatically after a few months.

Beginner default

For a small purchase of a popular stock or ETF during market hours, a market order is fine. For anything thinly traded, or when the market is closed, use a limit order so you don't get a surprise price.

Fractional shares

Many brokers let you buy part of a share by typing in a dollar amount. That's a fractional share. $25 of a $400 stock buys 25 ÷ 400 = 0.0625 shares. You get the same percent gains and losses as a whole share, plus a matching slice of any dividend.

The math you'll use constantly

Percent change(new price − old price) ÷ old price × 100

A stock goes from $40 to $46: (46 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = +15%.

Profit or loss(sell price − buy price) × number of shares

Gains and losses aren't symmetric. If a stock falls 50%, from $100 to $50, it has to rise 100% to get back to $100. Big losses are very hard to climb out of, which is one reason to avoid risky, concentrated bets.

One last detail: the cash and the shares officially swap one business day after your trade. That's called settlement, or T+1.

Lesson 4 of 14 · 12 min

Sizing up a company

By the end, you can use a handful of numbers and a checklist to judge whether a business is strong and whether its stock price is reasonable.

Buying a stock means buying part of a business. So the first question isn't "Will the price go up?" It's "Is this a good business, and am I paying a fair price for it?" Studying a company's actual business and finances is called fundamental analysis.

The income statement in one table

Meet Crust & Co., a made-up pizza chain.

Revenue (all money from sales)$2,000 million
Costs: ingredients, wages, rent, ads, taxes−$1,800 million
Net income (profit)$200 million
Shares outstanding100 million
  • Revenue is the "top line": everything the company sells.
  • Net income is the "bottom line": what's left after every cost and tax.
Profit marginnet income ÷ revenue = 200 ÷ 2,000 = 10%

Crust & Co. keeps 10 cents of every dollar of pizza it sells. That's its profit margin.

Earnings per share (EPS)net income ÷ shares = $200M ÷ 100M = $2.00

The P/E ratio: is the price reasonable?

Price-to-earningsP/E = share price ÷ EPS

If Crust & Co. stock costs $30, its P/E ratio is 30 ÷ 2 = 15. You're paying $15 for each $1 of yearly profit.

  • High P/E (say, 40 or more): investors expect fast growth. That can be justified, or it can mean the stock is overhyped.
  • Low P/E (say, under 12): investors expect slow growth or trouble. It can be a bargain, or a trap.
  • Compare P/Es only with similar companies in the same industry, and with the company's own history. A software company and a grocery chain normally have very different P/Es.
  • A company that loses money has no meaningful P/E, because its EPS is negative.

Dividend yield and payout ratio

Dividend yieldyearly dividend per share ÷ share price × 100

Crust & Co. pays $0.60 per share each year. At $30 a share, its dividend yield is 0.60 ÷ 30 = 2%. Own $1,000 of the stock and you'd get about $20 a year.

Its payout ratio is dividend ÷ EPS = 0.60 ÷ 2.00 = 30%. It pays out 30% of profits and keeps 70% to grow.

Watch out

A very high dividend yield, like 8% or more, is often a warning sign. Usually the price has crashed because investors expect the dividend to be cut.

The balance sheet: what it owns and owes

Balance sheetassets − liabilities = shareholders' equity

It works like a person's finances: what you own minus what you owe is what you're worth. A company with heavy debt gets into trouble fast when sales drop, because the loan payments don't stop.

The moat

Great businesses have something that keeps competitors away, like the moat around a castle.

Brand

People happily pay more for the name.

Network effects

The product gets better as more people use it, like messaging apps and marketplaces.

Switching costs

Leaving is a pain: your files, your settings, your habits.

Cost advantage

So big or efficient that it can charge less than anyone else.

Where to find the facts

  • The company's investor relations website: earnings reports and presentations.
  • SEC filings, free on EDGAR at sec.gov. The 10-K is the annual report and the 10-Q is the quarterly one. The "Business" and "Risk Factors" sections of a 10-K are very readable.
  • Your broker's stock page, which collects the key numbers in one place.

Watch out

A great company can still be a bad investment if you pay too much for it. Everyone already knows the famous companies are great, and that's usually built into the price.

The six-question checklist

  • Can I explain how this company makes money in two sentences?
  • Has revenue grown over the last three to five years?
  • Is it profitable, with steady or rising margins?
  • Is its debt manageable?
  • Does it have a moat?
  • Is the price reasonable compared with its earnings and its competitors?

Some people instead study price charts and patterns. That's called technical analysis, and it's all over social media. For long-term beginners, there's little evidence that it helps, and it pushes you to trade too often.

Lesson 5 of 14 · 9 min

Funds: hundreds of companies in one buy

By the end, you can explain index funds and ETFs, compare them by fees, and say why most investors build on them.

Picking individual winners is hard. A fund pools money from many investors and buys lots of stocks or bonds at once. One purchase gets you a slice of all of them.

Three words you'll see everywhere

Mutual fund

Bought and sold once a day, at a price set after the market closes. Some have minimum investments.

ETF

An exchange-traded fund. It has a ticker and trades all day like a stock. Many brokers sell fractional ETF shares.

Index fund

A mutual fund or ETF that copies an index, like the S&P 500, instead of paying managers to pick stocks.

Active vs. passive

Active investing means managers pick stocks, trying to beat the market, and charge higher fees for it. Passive investing means buying an index fund that just copies the market, for a very low fee.

Here's the surprising part. Most professional stock pickers don't beat the index over time. S&P's long-running SPIVA reports find that close to 9 out of 10 actively managed US large-company funds have trailed the S&P 500 over 15-year periods. Fees are a big reason why.

The expense ratio

Every fund charges a yearly fee called the expense ratio, a percent of the money you have in it. It's taken out automatically, so you never see a bill.

Expense ratioYearly fee on $10,000$10,000 after 40 years at 7%
0.03%$3about $148,000
0.20%$20about $139,000
1.00%$100about $103,000

A 1% fee sounds tiny. Over 40 years, it costs about $45,000 here, because money lost to fees also stops compounding.

Common types of funds

  • Total US stock market: nearly every public US company.
  • S&P 500: about 500 large US companies.
  • International: companies outside the US.
  • Total world: US and international in one fund.
  • Bond funds: many bonds. Steadier, with lower growth.
  • Target-date funds: a mix that gets more conservative as a target year, like 2070, gets closer. Common in retirement accounts.
  • Sector and theme funds: one industry or trend, like tech or clean energy. Less diversified, and often launched after the theme is already hot.

Before you buy any fund, check four things on its page: which index it tracks, its expense ratio, how many companies it holds, and its top 10 holdings. Because the S&P 500 is weighted by size, its biggest companies make up a large share of it. Even a broad index isn't perfectly balanced.

Watch out

Leveraged and inverse ETFs, often labeled 2x, 3x, "Ultra" or "Bear," use borrowing to multiply an index's daily move. They reset every day, so over weeks and months they can lose money even when the index ends up flat. They're built for short-term traders, not for you.

Lesson 6 of 14 · 10 min

Risk and your brain

By the end, you know how far stocks can fall, how diversification and time reduce risk, and which mental traps cause bad decisions.

Risk in investing means two things: how much prices swing, and the chance you lose money you can't afford to lose.

Big drops are normal

These are real declines of the S&P 500, from its peak to its lowest point.

EventDropBack to old high (from the peak)
Dot-com crash, 2000–2002about −49%about 7 years
Financial crisis, 2007–2009about −57%about 5½ years
COVID crash, Feb–Mar 2020about −34%about 6 months
Inflation and rate hikes, 2022about −25%about 2 years

Prices only, not counting dividends. Each time, the market eventually reached new highs. But look at the range: sometimes months, sometimes years. That's why one rule matters so much:

Rule

Money you'll need within about 5 years, for a car, college costs or a trip, belongs in savings, not stocks. How long until you need the money is your time horizon.

Diversification

Single companies can go to zero. Enron (2001), Lehman Brothers (2008) and Blockbuster (2010) were all big, famous companies that went bankrupt. Diversification means spreading your money across many investments so one disaster can't sink you.

  • Company-specific risk hits one business: a scandal, a failed product. Diversification can mostly remove it.
  • Market risk hits nearly everything at once, like a recession. Diversification can't remove it. Time is how you handle it.

Dollar-cost averaging

Dollar-cost averaging means investing the same amount on a schedule, like $50 every month, whatever the price. You automatically buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when they're high.

MonthPriceYou investShares bought
1$10$505.00
2$8$506.25
3$5$5010.00

Your average cost is $250 ÷ 32.5 = $7.69 a share, below the $8.20 average price. The price ended right where it started, at $10, but your 32.5 shares are worth $325, a 30% gain. Dollar-cost averaging also removes the stress of guessing the "right time" to buy.

Your brain is the biggest risk

FOMO

Buying because a stock already shot up and everyone's talking about it.

Panic selling

Selling after a big drop, locking in the loss right before a possible rebound.

Loss aversion

Losses feel about twice as bad as equal gains feel good, so we make odd choices to avoid them.

Confirmation bias

Only reading opinions that agree with what you already believe.

Overconfidence

A few lucky wins make you feel like a genius, so you bet bigger.

Recency bias

Assuming whatever just happened will keep happening.

Time in the market beats timing the market. Some of the market's best days have come right after its worst ones, so people who panic-sell often miss the rebound.

Write this rule down

"I won't sell an investment just because its price dropped. I'll sell only if the reason I bought it is no longer true, or I need the money for a planned goal."

Compound growth: time is your edge

By the end, you'll see why starting at 16 matters more than starting with a lot of money, and you can run your own numbers.

Compound growth means your gains start earning gains of their own. Simple interest pays you only on what you put in. Compounding pays you on everything, including past growth.

Two habits that keep compounding going

  • Reinvest dividends. Turn on dividend reinvestment (often called DRIP) at your broker, so dividends automatically buy more shares.
  • Don't interrupt it. Every time you pull money out or sell in a panic, you reset part of the snowball.

Inflation is why this matters. Prices rise over time (the Federal Reserve aims for about 2% a year), so cash sitting still slowly buys less. Your real return is what you earn after inflation. Investing is how your money keeps up and gets ahead.

Lesson 8 of 14 · 11 min

Starting with limited money

By the end, you know which accounts a teen can use, what to do before investing, and a simple plan for $10 to $100 a month.

Where this applies

The account rules below are for the US. In other countries, account types and age limits are different. Look up your local options with a parent or guardian.

Before you invest a single dollar

  1. Keep some cash in savings for surprises, like a phone repair or a school trip. A high-yield savings account at an FDIC-insured bank pays more than a regular one.
  2. Only invest money you won't need for at least 5 years.
  3. Never invest borrowed money.
  4. Never invest because someone online promised fast returns.

Accounts you can use under 18

In the US, you generally need to be 18 to open a regular brokerage account in your own name. Until then, you have three main options.

Custodial account (UTMA/UGMA)

A parent or guardian opens and manages it for you. The money legally belongs to you, and you take control at 18 or 21, depending on your state. Money put in is a gift and can't be taken back.

Youth or teen account

Some brokers offer accounts for ages 13 to 17 that you can run yourself while a parent watches over it. Fidelity's Youth Account is one example. Compare what's available with a parent.

Custodial Roth IRA

If you earn money from work, you can contribute up to what you earned that year, up to the yearly IRA limit the IRS sets. The money grows tax-free, and withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

Earned income can come from a regular job, but also babysitting, mowing lawns or tutoring, as long as you keep records. A Roth IRA is a big deal for teens: you probably pay little or no income tax now, and you'd never pay tax on decades of growth. You can withdraw your contributions (not the growth) at any time, but the account works best left alone.

Finding money to invest

Part-time jobs, allowance, birthday money, selling things you've made. Even $10 a week is $520 a year. At this stage, small amounts matter mostly because they build the habit early, and the habit is what compounds.

A starter plan for $10 to $100 a month

  1. Core, 80–100%: one broad, low-fee index fund or ETF, such as a total US market, S&P 500 or total world fund.
  2. Learning slice, 0–20%: individual companies you've researched with the Lesson 4 checklist. Treat any losses here as tuition.
  3. Automate it: set up an automatic monthly investment so you don't have to decide each time.
  4. Buy fractional shares so every dollar gets invested.
  5. Turn on dividend reinvestment.
  6. Keep a journal: what you bought, why, and what would make you sell.
  7. Check monthly, not hourly.

Practice with fake money first

Paper trading means making pretend trades with fake money to learn how orders and price swings feel. Many brokers and school stock market games offer simulators. Try one for a month, and make your mistakes there for free.

One caution: fake money makes it easy to take wild risks you'd never take with real money. Practice the way you actually plan to invest.

Taxes in one minute (US)

  • In a regular account, you owe tax on a gain only when you sell. Until then it's unrealized.
  • Held one year or less: a short-term capital gain, taxed like regular income. Held more than one year: a long-term capital gain, usually taxed at a lower rate.
  • Dividends are usually taxed in the year you get them, even if they're reinvested.
  • Your broker sends a Form 1099 each year. A parent can help you file.
  • A child's investment income above a set amount can be taxed at the parent's rate. This is called the "kiddie tax."
  • Inside a Roth IRA, none of this applies while the money stays in.

Lesson 9 of 14 · 9 min

Traps, scams and things to skip for now

By the end, you can spot the products and pitches that cause beginners their biggest losses.

Skip these for now

Most lose money

Day trading

Buying and selling within the same day to catch small moves. One large study of Brazilian day traders found that 97% of those who kept at it for over 300 days lost money. You're up against professionals and computers.

Can go to zero fast

Options

Contracts to buy or sell a stock at a set price by a set date. Many expire worthless. Generally not allowed in custodial accounts anyway.

Lose more than you put in

Margin

Borrowing from your broker to buy more. Gains and losses both get bigger. If your account falls too far, the broker can sell your stocks for you in a margin call.

No ceiling on losses

Short selling

Borrowing shares and selling them, betting the price will fall. If it rises instead, your loss can keep growing.

Easy to manipulate

Penny stocks

Very cheap stocks, often traded "over the counter" with little public information. Thin trading makes them easy to push around.

Hype-driven

Meme stocks

Stocks that soar on social media buzz. GameStop rose more than tenfold in a few weeks in January 2021, then lost most of that gain within weeks. Many late buyers lost most of their money.

Scam red flags

Scammers target young investors on social media, Discord and group chats. Watch for:

  • Guaranteed or "can't lose" returns, like "10% a week."
  • Pressure to act now: "only 3 spots left," "last chance."
  • A secret strategy, signals group or paid course that promises riches.
  • Anyone asking for your login or password, or asking you to send money to their account or crypto wallet.
  • Pump and dump: a group hypes a tiny stock so its price jumps, then the organizers sell to the people they convinced, and the price collapses.
  • Screenshots of huge gains and flashy lifestyles, which are easy to fake.
  • Fake accounts pretending to be celebrities or real advisors.

Where to check

Real investing is mostly boring. Anyone promising fast, sure money is either wrong or lying. You can look up whether a financial professional is registered at FINRA BrokerCheck (brokercheck.finra.org), and learn more at Investor.gov, run by the SEC.

Crypto and apps that feel like games

Crypto coins are not stocks. They don't own a business or earn profits, and their prices can swing wildly. Many scams aimed at teens involve crypto. If you get curious later, keep it to a small amount you could afford to lose completely.

Be just as careful with apps that make trading feel like a game, with confetti, streaks and push alerts. They're built to make you trade more, and trading more usually means worse results.

Influencers

Finance influencers can teach useful things. But many are paid to promote products, or earn money when you sign up through their link. Before you follow anyone's advice, ask two questions: How does this person make money? What happens to me if they're wrong?

Reading a price chart

By the end, you can read a stock chart, spot the ways charts mislead, and tell useful information from noise.

A price chart shows price over time: time runs along the bottom and price runs up the side. Every broker app opens with one, so it's worth knowing what a chart can and can't tell you.

Line charts and candlesticks

A line chart connects the closing prices. A candlestick chart shows four prices for each period, such as each day: the open, the close, the high and the low.

  • The thick body runs from the open to the close.
  • The thin lines, called wicks, reach the period's high and low.
  • Green means the price closed higher than it opened. Red means it closed lower.

Many charts also show volume bars along the bottom. A very tall bar means unusually heavy trading, often because of news.

Moving averages

A moving average is the average closing price over a set number of recent days, recalculated every day. It smooths out the zigzags so you can see the trend. The 50-day and 200-day averages are the most common. Turn on "Moving average" in the chart above to see one. It describes the past. It doesn't predict anything.

Five ways charts mislead

  1. Short timeframes exaggerate noise. A scary one-day drop can be a blip on a five-year chart.
  2. Squeezed price axes make small moves look huge. Compare the two charts below.
  3. Normal scales distort long periods. On a normal (linear) scale, a 10% move at $1,000 looks ten times taller than a 10% move at $100. A log scale draws equal percent moves at equal heights, so long histories look fair.
  4. Cherry-picked start dates. "Up 400%!" often means "measured from its lowest point ever."
  5. Price-only charts leave out dividends. A dividend stock's chart understates what its owners actually earned.

Patterns and predictions

Social media is full of chart patterns with names like "head and shoulders," and lines called support and resistance. Reading charts to predict prices is technical analysis (Lesson 4). For long-term beginners there's little evidence it works, and it pushes you to trade more. A chart tells you what happened, not what happens next.

What to actually look at

  • The five-year or longest view, to see the long-term trend.
  • The biggest drop from a high in that period, called the drawdown. Could you have held on through it?
  • How it compares with the S&P 500 over the same period. Many apps let you add a comparison line.
  • Whether a big jump or drop lines up with real news, like an earnings report.

Lesson 11 of 14 · 10 min

The economy and stocks

By the end, you can explain how interest rates, inflation, jobs and recessions move stock prices, and translate a financial headline.

Over the long run, stock prices follow company profits, and profits depend on the economy. When people have jobs, they spend. When they spend, companies sell more. About two-thirds of the US economy is spending by ordinary people.

The numbers investors watch

NumberWhat it measuresWho reports it
GDPThe total value of everything the economy produces. Growth means the economy is expanding.Bureau of Economic Analysis, every quarter
Unemployment rateThe share of people looking for work who can't find a job.Bureau of Labor Statistics, monthly jobs report
CPIInflation: how fast prices of everyday goods and services are rising.Bureau of Labor Statistics, monthly
Federal funds rateThe interest rate banks charge each other overnight. It steers most other rates.The Federal Reserve, at meetings about every six weeks

The Fed's job

The Federal Reserve, or "the Fed," is the US central bank. Congress gave it two goals: as many jobs as possible, and stable prices. It aims for inflation of about 2% a year.

  • When inflation runs too hot, the Fed raises interest rates. Borrowing gets more expensive, spending slows, and price increases cool down.
  • When the economy weakens, the Fed cuts rates to make borrowing cheaper and encourage spending.

Why interest rates move stocks

  1. Borrowing costs. Higher rates make loans pricier for companies and their customers, so both spend and expand less.
  2. Competition. When a savings account pays 5%, a risky stock looks less appealing than when savings pay 0.5%.
  3. Future profits are worth less today. Companies expected to earn most of their profits far in the future get hit hardest. In 2022, as the Fed raised rates quickly, the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite fell about a third.

Rule of thumb

Rates up, stocks often down. Rates down, stocks often up. It's a tendency, not a law.

Recessions

A recession is a broad decline in the economy that lasts months, with falling output and rising unemployment. People often say "two quarters in a row of shrinking GDP," but in the US a committee of economists at the National Bureau of Economic Research makes the official call. The US has had about a dozen recessions since World War II, including 2001, 2007–2009 and a short one in 2020.

Stocks often fall before and during a recession, and often start recovering before it officially ends. The market looks ahead, so prices can rise while the news is still bad.

Different companies, different reactions

Defensive

Utilities, groceries, healthcare, household goods. People buy these in any economy, so profits hold up better.

Cyclical

Airlines, cars, restaurants, luxury goods. Sales rise and fall with the economy.

Rate-sensitive

Banks, real estate, homebuilders. Their business depends on borrowing, so interest rates hit them directly.

Decode the headline

Financial news uses the same few ideas over and over. Tap each headline to translate it. These are practice headlines written in news style, not quotes from real articles.

Should you trade on economic news?

No. By the time a report reaches your phone, professional traders have already reacted, usually within seconds. Economists also disagree with each other, and recession predictions are often wrong. Use the news to understand why your account moved, not as a signal to buy or sell.

What should change your plan

Your goals, your time horizon or your income. Not the news.

Lesson 12 of 14 · 9 min

Budgeting: finding money to invest

By the end, you can read a paycheck, build a monthly budget, and decide how much to invest.

You can't invest money you've already spent. A budget is a plan that gives every dollar a job before the month starts, so you're not left wondering where it went.

Read your paycheck

Your first paycheck will be smaller than hours × wage. Gross pay is what you earned. Net pay is what lands in your account after deductions.

Needs, wants and goals

  • Needs are what you must pay for, like the phone bill you agreed to cover or gas to get to work.
  • Wants are nice to have, like eating out, games and streaming.
  • Goals are saving and investing for the future.

Fixed expenses stay the same each month, like a phone plan. Variable expenses change, like food and gas. Variable expenses are where you have the most control.

Pay yourself first

On payday, move your savings and investing money first, ideally automatically. Then spend what's left. If you wait to invest whatever is left over, there usually isn't anything left.

Adults often use a 50/30/20 split: 50% needs, 30% wants, 20% saving. Teens living at home usually have fewer needs, so 50% spending, 40% saving and investing and 10% giving is a common place to start. These are starting points, not rules.

Build your budget

The example below is filled in for a $400-a-month weekend job. Change any number to match your life. It's saved in this browser.

Your emergency fund

An emergency fund is cash for surprises, kept in a savings account. Adults aim for 3 to 6 months of expenses. For a teen, a few hundred dollars, or about one month of spending, is a good first target. It protects your investments: a broken phone never forces you to sell stocks during a crash.

Debt comes first

Credit card interest rates are often 20% a year or more. Stocks have averaged about 10%. If you carry a card balance, paying it off is like earning a guaranteed 20%. The yearly cost of borrowing is shown as an APR. "Buy now, pay later" plans are borrowing too, even when they feel like a payment plan.

Small leaks

A $12 monthly subscription costs $144 a year. Invested at 7% a year from age 16 to 65 instead, $12 a month could grow to about $60,800. That's no reason to never have fun. It's a reason to choose on purpose.

Check your budget once a month. Compare what you planned with what you actually spent, and adjust.

Beyond stocks

By the end, you know how bonds, cash, real estate, gold and crypto work, and how investors mix them.

Stocks are one asset class: a group of investments that behave in similar ways. Here are the main ones.

AssetWhat you ownHow it makes moneyRisk
CashSavings, CDs, money market fundsInterestVery low
BondsA loan to a government or companyInterest, plus your money back at the endLow to medium
StocksPart of a companyProfits, through price growth and dividendsHigh
Real estateShares of companies that own propertyRent, paid out as dividendsMedium to high
GoldA physical metalOnly if someone pays more laterHigh, no income
CryptoA digital tokenOnly if someone pays more laterVery high, no income

Bonds, up close

A bond is a loan with three parts: the face value (what you get back), the coupon (the yearly interest rate) and the maturity (when the loan ends).

  • Treasury bonds are loans to the US government. They're considered among the safest investments in the world.
  • Corporate bonds are loans to companies. They pay more because the company could fail. Bonds from shaky companies are called high-yield or "junk" bonds.

Watch out

Bond prices move opposite to interest rates. If new bonds pay 5%, nobody will pay full price for your 4% bond, so its price falls. In 2022, when rates rose fast, the broad US bond market lost about 13%, its worst year on record.

Cash that earns something

  • High-yield savings account: FDIC-insured, and you can withdraw any time.
  • CD (certificate of deposit): you lock money up for a set time, like one year, at a fixed rate. Taking it out early costs a penalty.
  • Money market fund: a fund that holds very short-term loans. Very low risk, but not FDIC-insured.

Real estate, gold and crypto

A REIT (real estate investment trust) owns income-producing property, like apartments, warehouses or cell towers, and must pay out most of its taxable income as dividends. You can buy REITs through a fund, and a total stock market fund already includes some.

Gold and other commodities don't produce profits or pay anything. Their price depends on what the next buyer will pay. Gold sometimes holds up in a crisis, but over long periods it has grown much less than stocks.

Cryptocurrency like Bitcoin also produces no profits or income. Bitcoin has fallen more than 70% from a peak several times. US bitcoin ETFs started trading in 2024, which made it easier to buy, not less risky. If you ever own any, keep it to a small amount you could lose completely.

Asset allocation: your mix

Your asset allocation is how you split your money between asset classes. It's the biggest single decision for how bumpy your ride will be. Pick a mix to compare.

More stocks means more growth on average, and deeper worst years. With 40+ years ahead and income from work, most young investors hold mostly or all stocks. People close to retirement add bonds because they can't wait years for a recovery. Target-date funds (Lesson 5) shift the mix for you automatically.

Rebalancing

Over time your mix drifts. If you want 80% stocks and a great year pushes you to 90%, rebalancing means moving back to 80%, either by selling some stocks and buying bonds or by sending new money to bonds. Once a year is plenty.

Lesson 14 of 14 · 10 min

Famous mistakes

By the end, you can name the few mistakes behind most investing disasters, and how to avoid each one.

Most investing disasters come from the same few mistakes: overpaying for a story, putting too much in one place, borrowing, panicking, and trusting the wrong people. Here they are in real life, in order.

  1. 1995–2002

    The dot-com bubble

    What happenedInvestors poured money into new internet companies, many with little revenue and no profit, because "the internet changes everything." The Nasdaq Composite peaked in March 2000, then fell about 78% by October 2002. Pets.com went from its stock market debut to shutting down in about nine months. Even Amazon, a huge long-term winner, fell more than 90%.

    The mistakePaying any price for a great story. The internet really did change everything, but most of those stocks were priced as if every company would win. This kind of hype-driven price surge is called a bubble.

    The lessonA world-changing idea doesn't make every stock a good buy. Price matters: the Nasdaq took about 15 years to get back to its 2000 peak. Lesson 4

  2. 1998

    Long-Term Capital Management

    What happenedA hedge fund run by star Wall Street traders, with two Nobel Prize-winning economists as partners, borrowed huge amounts to magnify small bets its models called safe. When Russia defaulted on its debt in 1998, markets moved in ways the models said were nearly impossible. The fund lost about $4.6 billion in a few months, and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York organized a rescue by major banks.

    The mistakeLeverage plus overconfidence.

    The lessonBorrowing turns ordinary losses into catastrophic ones, no matter how smart you are. Lesson 9

  3. 2001

    Enron

    What happenedEnron was one of America's biggest companies and a Wall Street favorite. It used accounting tricks to hide debt and make profits look bigger. When the truth came out, the stock fell from about $90 to under $1 in a little over a year, and the company went bankrupt in December 2001. Many employees had most of their retirement savings in Enron stock, so they lost their jobs and their savings at the same time.

    The mistakeConcentration, especially in your own employer.

    The lessonDiversify. Never let one company, even a famous one, hold most of your money. Lesson 6

  4. 2007–2009

    The financial crisis

    What happenedBanks and homebuyers borrowed heavily, believing home prices couldn't fall across the whole country. When they did, loans went bad. Lehman Brothers went bankrupt in September 2008, and the S&P 500 fell about 57% from its peak. Many investors sold near the bottom in early 2009 and missed one of the strongest recoveries in history.

    The mistakeToo much debt, then panic selling.

    The lessonLeverage magnifies losses, and selling in a panic turns a temporary drop into a permanent loss. Lesson 6

  5. 2008

    Bernie Madoff

    What happenedMadoff, a former chairman of the Nasdaq stock market, ran the largest Ponzi scheme in history: he paid "returns" to old clients with new clients' money. His statements showed smooth gains of about 1% a month, year after year, even in bad markets. When the 2008 crash brought a wave of withdrawals, it collapsed. The fake account statements added up to about $65 billion.

    The mistakeTrusting returns that were too smooth, and a famous name.

    The lessonReal investments go up and down. Steady, too-good returns are a red flag. Check registration and who actually holds your money. Lesson 9

  6. 2021

    GameStop

    What happenedTraders on Reddit piled into GameStop, a struggling video game retailer that big hedge funds had bet against through short selling. The price rose more than tenfold in a few weeks. One hedge fund that had shorted it lost billions and later shut down. Then the price fell most of the way back within weeks, and many people who bought near the top lost most of their money.

    The mistakeBoth sides. The short sellers took on unlimited risk, and late buyers chased FOMO.

    The lessonHype cuts both ways. When everyone is talking about a stock, the easy money has usually already been made. Lesson 9

  7. 2022

    FTX

    What happenedFTX was one of the world's biggest crypto exchanges, promoted by celebrities and athletes and even named on a sports arena. In November 2022 it collapsed within days when it came out that customer deposits had been secretly used for risky bets. Its founder was convicted of fraud in 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in prison.

    The mistakeTrusting celebrity endorsements, and keeping money somewhere you couldn't check how it was held.

    The lessonFame isn't proof. Use regulated, well-known brokers, and look up anyone selling you an investment. Lesson 9
